# Strategies for Executing Analytics Projects: Toward a Unified Framework of Methodologies


**ABSTRACT**


Analytics-project methodologies remain fragmented across technical, managerial, and governance perspectives.
This study systematically identifies and analyzes 18 methodological documents representing analytics-project
methodologies, extensions, and comparative syntheses to develop PRODIG8, a unified model for analytics-project
execution. The model was derived through iterative structural reconciliation, sequentially comparing methodologies
and modifying the evolving architecture by adding, renaming, merging, or splitting methodological functions. The 18
methodologies were then remapped to the resulting structure and their recommendations consolidated. PRODIG8
comprises eight methodological dimensions organized into three functional components: six core execution
dimensions, Governance and Ethics as a transversal control dimension, and Continuous Improvement as an adaptive
feedback dimension. This execution–control–adaptation architecture integrates technical execution, organizational
governance, and continuous learning within a single model. PRODIG8 provides a systematically derived analytical
structure for organizing, comparing, and refining analytics-project practices while preserving adaptability across
organizational contexts.


**Keywords**

Business Analytics; Methodology; Governance; CRISP-DM; TDSP; Agile Analytics; Continuous Improvement.

**1** **Introduction**


Over recent decades, the digitalization of processes and the expansion of analytical capabilities have profoundly
reshaped how organizations operate, compete, and make decisions (Davenport & Harris, 2007; Provost & Fawcett,
2013). In this dynamic environment, the ability to collect, analyze, and interpret large volumes of information has
become a strategic asset, enabling firms to optimize processes, anticipate market trends, and foster innovation
(Davenport & Harris, 2007; Provost & Fawcett, 2013). Accordingly, analytics is no longer an auxiliary function but a
central driver of value creation and long-term competitiveness (Provost & Fawcett, 2013). The increasing development
of business analytics solutions within organizations reflects the exponential growth, diversity, and complexity of data
generated by digital transformation, the Internet of Things (IoT), Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS), and advances in
artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) (Azeroual et al., 2025; Bemthuis et al., 2025; Fayyad et al.,
2000; Huber et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2025).

Despite technological advances, the organizational landscape of analytics adoption remains complex. Many
initiatives fail to deliver measurable value (Martinez-Plumed et al., 2021) due to persistent barriers, including
heterogeneous data sources, data quality management, governance, privacy, and compliance with evolving legal
standards (Azeroual et al., 2025; Ma et al., 2025; Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025; Wirth & Hipp, 2000). Recent studies
report that approximately 87% of data science projects never reach production, and that over 80% of organizations
lack a formal methodology for analytics execution (Saltz & Krasteva, 2022). Moreover, analytics projects are hindered
by socio-technical and managerial obstacles, including siloed coordination among multidisciplinary teams, weak
change-management practices, and limited stakeholder engagement (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Hamdani et al., 2024;
Petrin et al., 2025; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). These issues are compounded by the misalignment between rapidly
expanding data capabilities and organizations’ analytical maturity, leading to underutilization of valuable data and
fragmented project execution (Azeroual et al., 2025; Fayyad et al., 2000).

The diversity of project contexts and disciplinary origins has produced a wide range of methodological
frameworks—such as KDD (Angée et al., 2018; Martinez et al., 2021; Saltz & Krasteva, 2022; Schröer et al., 2021),
CRISP-DM (Fayyad et al., 1996a; Matignon, 2007; Wirth & Hipp, 2000, 2000), and SEMMA ( _CRISP-DM 1.0_, 2000;
Fayyad et al., 1996b; Schröer et al., 2021)—each valuable yet limited in adaptability, contextualization, and
governance. In response, newer frameworks, including ASUM-DM (Agrawal & Shafer, 1996), TDSP (Microsoft
Corporation, 2016), CASP-DM (Martinez-Plumed et al., 2021), and OSEMN (Bhardwaj, 2020), have emerged,
incorporating agile and project-management principles. Complementing these, the INFORMS Analytics Process
(INFORMS, 2014) provides a professional standard of practice widely adopted in industry, outlining seven stages—
from business problem framing to lifecycle management—that link analytical rigor with decision-making and
governance. The Data Mining Methodology for Engineering Applications (DMME) (Huber et al., 2019) further
extends CRISP-DM by adding three technical phases—data acquisition, sensor integration, and engineering
experimentation—that link measurement systems and analytical modeling in cyber-physical production environments.
Hybrid implementations, such as those combining CRISP-DM with agile Scrum practices in supply-chain analytics
(Hamdani et al., 2024), demonstrate how iterative sprints and user feedback loops improve responsiveness, team
coordination, and socio-technical integration within complex analytics projects. In small and medium-sized
enterprises, where analytical teams and technical resources are typically limited, agile approaches such as Scrum and
XP offer lightweight, iterative alternatives that enhance adaptability, stakeholder communication, and incremental
value delivery within analytics projects (Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). Nevertheless, methodological fragmentation
persists; studies often analyze these models in isolation, rarely comparing or integrating them (Fois et al., 2020; SAS
Institute Inc., 2018). This lack of consolidation results in inefficiencies, inconsistent project execution, and reduced
replicability of outcomes (Angée et al., 2018; Fois et al., 2020; Martinez-Plumed et al., 2021; Montoya-Murillo et al.,
2025; Plotnikova et al., 2020; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). Therefore, an integrative synthesis is required to unify
these dispersed approaches and provide a comprehensive methodological foundation for the execution of analytics
projects.

This study addresses this fragmentation by systematically identifying and integrating methodologies for executing
analytics projects and deriving, from their cumulative methodological contributions, a unified analytical model of
analytics-project execution. Unlike prior reviews that primarily compare or classify existing approaches (Martinez
et al., 2021; Saltz & Krasteva, 2022), the study reconstructs their methodological lineage and examines how successive
frameworks progressively differentiate and extend the functions required for project execution. This evolution
encompasses advances in project management and agility (Hamdani et al., 2024; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024),
domain-specific technical requirements (Huber et al., 2019), governance and organizational integration (MontoyaMurillo et al., 2025), and iterative learning and operational adaptation (Bokrantz et al., 2024). Recent adaptations of

Before presenting the methodological workflow, it is essential to situate the unified framework within the broader
theoretical lineage of analytics methodologies. The following section, therefore, reviews the main paradigms that have
shaped the execution of analytics projects, tracing how successive generations of process models have progressively
integrated governance, ethics, and iterative learning.


**2** **Theoretical Framework**


The execution of analytics projects has evolved through successive methodological paradigms, closely mirroring
technological progress and organizational transformation. Each paradigm reflects the technological and organizational
conditions of its time. Foundational models such as Knowledge Discovery in Databases (KDD) (Fayyad et al., 1996b),
CRISP-DM (Chapman et al., 2000), and SEMMA (SAS Institute Inc., 1998) established the procedural basis for
structured data mining and analytics practice. These frameworks share a sequential logic—progressing from problem
definition and data preparation to modeling, evaluation, and deployment—that provided early standardization for
analytical workflows. Their principal contribution lies in defining reproducible stages and deliverables, yet their tooloriented design and limited attention to governance and adaptability restrict their suitability for contemporary,
dynamic environments. Despite these limitations, they remain the conceptual backbone of modern analytics
methodologies, establishing the sequential logic and standardized deliverables that later frameworks—such as ASUMDM, TDSP, DMME, and DataPro—would refine through iterative, agile, and governance-oriented extensions.
Beyond its structural clarity, CRISP-DM remains historically significant as the first methodology to formalize a
standard, repeatable, and technology-agnostic process model for data mining, introducing iterative feedback loops and
documentation practices that continue to underpin subsequent frameworks, including those integrated in this study’s
unified model. In addition, domain-specific adaptations, particularly in education and academic management, have
expanded CRISP-DM through process mining integrations that enable curriculum evaluation and predictive student
performance analytics, demonstrating its cross-domain versatility (Herrera Franco et al., 2025).

Recent adaptations have further evolved CRISP-DM toward quality assurance and lifecycle governance. Notably,
CRISP-ML(Q) (Studer et al., 2021) extends CRISP-DM by incorporating explicit quality management,
reproducibility, and long-term model maintenance steps, reflecting an increasing convergence between
methodological rigor and operational reliability. These extensions echo the same need for continuous monitoring and
improvement that underpins the eighth phase of the unified framework proposed in this study.

Subsequent frameworks, such as ASUM-DM (IBM, 2015) and TDSP (Microsoft Corporation, 2016), expanded
on these classical models by integrating principles from software engineering and agile project management. ASUMDM introduced lifecycle documentation, stakeholder engagement mechanisms, and process iteration, while TDSP
emphasized automation, version control, and continuous integration. Developed by IBM as an evolution of CRISPDM, ASUM-DM explicitly integrates project and communication management activities to coordinate multiorganizational analytics initiatives and to align technical execution with governance and stakeholder requirements.

Similarly, TDSP aligns analytical development with DevOps principles, emphasizing automation and reproducibility
across collaborative teams. Recent hybrid methodologies reinforce that combining CRISP-DM’s structured phases
with agile’s iterative and human-centered practices enhances collaboration, responsiveness, and alignment between
technical execution and organizational learning (Hamdani et al., 2024). Agile-oriented methodologies typically
emphasize seven recurring attributes—flexibility, responsiveness, speed, lean development, simplicity, lightweight
documentation, and limited formal deliverables—which underpin the adaptive behavior now integrated into modern
analytics frameworks.

Complementing these research- and vendor-oriented methodologies, the INFORMS Analytics Process
(INFORMS, 2014) represents a practitioner-standard framework endorsed by the Institute for Operations Research
and the Management Sciences. It structures analytics projects into seven sequential yet iterative stages—business
problem framing, analytics problem framing, data, methodology, model building, deployment, and lifecycle
management—providing a governance-oriented bridge between business objectives and technical execution.
Designed originally for professional certification under the Certified Analytics Professional (CAP) program, the
INFORMS framework operationalizes communication, accountability, and value realization across project phases. Its
emphasis on traceability and stakeholder alignment directly supports the governance-aware orientation of the unified
framework proposed in this study.

Similarly, methodologies such as DMME, MAISTRO, and DataPro advanced socio-technical considerations,
embedding elements of collaboration, ethics, and organizational governance into the analytics lifecycle. DMME
(Huber et al., 2019), in particular, extends CRISP-DM by introducing three additional phases—Technical
Understanding, Technical Realization, and Technical Implementation—that explicitly address data acquisition, sensor
integration, and process alignment in engineering environments. These three phases bridge analytical modeling with
real-time data acquisition, connecting business objectives to operational feasibility in industrial and cyber-physical
contexts. Each phase corresponds to a specific gap in CRISP-DM: Technical Understanding adds requirements for
parameter definition and experimental planning; Technical Realization formalizes sensor installation and data
collection procedures; and Technical Implementation ensures that acquired data flows into operational analytics
systems through runtime integration and validation. This layered extension was empirically validated in industrial
testbeds and pilot projects, confirming its applicability for integrating analytical models within engineering and
production systems (Huber et al., 2019). Collectively, these frameworks illustrate a methodological shift from static,
sequential pipelines toward dynamic, iterative, and governance-aware lifecycles that reflect the growing complexity
and interdisciplinarity of analytics projects. Despite these advances, the literature continues to report persistent
challenges, including methodological fragmentation, weak interoperability among process models, and insufficient
standardization of roles, responsibilities, and quality metrics (Martinez et al., 2021; Saltz & Krasteva, 2022).

Within this evolving landscape, analyses such as Shimaoka et al. (2024) highlight the proliferation of domainspecific extensions that lack a unifying methodological standard, reinforcing the need for consolidation and crossdomain integration. Their findings reveal that while specialized frameworks have advanced practice within individual
sectors, the absence of a holistic structure limits interoperability and knowledge transfer across domains—precisely
the gap this study seeks to address.

Comparative reviews show that these frameworks converge conceptually around five foundational principles: (1)
structured phases that translate business problems into analytical tasks; (2) iteration and feedback as mechanisms for
learning; (3) cross-functional collaboration; (4) emphasis on data quality and reproducibility; and (5) increasing
concern for ethical and governance dimensions. Nonetheless, divergences persist in phase granularity, governance
integration, and treatment of post-deployment activities. Some models (e.g., CRISP-DM, SEMMA) end at
deployment, whereas newer approaches (e.g., TDSP, MAISTRO) extend to continuous operation and maintenance.
Others differ in their approach to ethics, transparency, and accountability—dimensions that are often external to early
process models but are now central to responsible analytics practice. OSEMN, widely adopted in real-time and applied
data science contexts, exemplifies the operational simplicity and adaptability that many modern methodologies,
including the unified framework proposed here, seek to institutionalize.

Across these successive paradigms, the methodological landscape reveals both conceptual continuity and
fragmentation. Existing reviews (Martinez et al., 2021; Saltz & Krasteva, 2022) underscore the absence of a
comprehensive framework that consolidates methodological diversity while embedding governance, adaptability, and
standardization. Theoretical gaps remain concerning (a) the integration of governance and ethics across all phases; (b)
the formalization of iterative learning cycles that connect technical and managerial processes; and (c) the definition
of cross-phase feedback mechanisms ensuring methodological coherence. Addressing these gaps requires
reconceptualizing analytics methodologies not as fixed pipelines but as adaptive systems that unify technical,
managerial, and ethical layers within a single structure.

The model proposed in this study responds directly to these theoretical gaps by integrating agile practices with
structured lifecycle approaches, consistent with recent calls for hybrid methodologies in analytics execution (Saltz &
Krasteva, 2022). It consolidates the principal methodological advances identified across successive frameworks—
standardization, iterative development, operationalization, governance, and continuous learning—within a
differentiated architecture. In this architecture, the core execution dimensions provide the structured lifecycle required
for systematic project development; Governance and Ethics operates as a transversal control mechanism; and
Continuous Improvement provides the adaptive feedback mechanism through which results, operational experience,
and changing conditions can reshape execution activities. This execution–control–adaptation structure aligns with the
growing emphasis on methodological maturity, accountability, and ethical responsibility reflected in standards such
as ISO/IEC 29110 and IEEE 7000. PRODIG8 therefore conceptualizes analytics-project management as an integrated
socio-technical system in which structure, control, and adaptation perform distinct but interdependent functions.


**3** **Materials and methods**


This section outlines the workflow adopted for the systematic literature analysis, following established
methodological frameworks (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017; Donthu et al., 2021; Page et al., 2021). The procedure is
structured into three stages: Section 2.1 outlines the study design, Section 2.2 describes data collection and preparation,
and Section 2.3 details the analytical construction of PRODIG8. All procedures, inclusion criteria, and coding schemes
were documented in structured spreadsheets to ensure transparency and replicability. The citation-expansion stage
identified the methodological corpus and traced its lineage, while the subsequent sequential structural-reconciliation
procedure derived the unified architecture. Once the final structure was established, the recommendations of all 18
methodologies were remapped and consolidated within its dimensions. This separation between corpus identification,
structural derivation, and recommendation consolidation provides the methodological basis for the PRODIG8 model.


**3.1** **Study design**


The methodological design of this study followed the principles for systematic reviews proposed by Kitchenham
(2004) and Page et al. (2021) and was grounded in an iterative, citation-based discovery strategy developed to trace
the evolution of methodologies for executing analytics projects. Rather than relying on topic-based keyword searches,
we employed a forward-citation expansion approach to identify methodological innovations by tracing their reference
relationships to established frameworks. This approach is efficient for capturing new or derivative methodologies
because it follows the field’s intellectual lineage, revealing conceptual extensions and refinements that may not share
common terminology. Although this method may not capture independently developed methodologies lacking citation
ties to foundational models, Saltz and Krasteva (2022) indicate that most contemporary frameworks are, in fact,
derivations of classical models, suggesting broad coverage. By recursively tracing citation linkages, this design
ensures completeness through saturation of methodological descendants. At the same time, reliability is strengthened
by limiting inclusion to peer-reviewed frameworks that formalize process models rather than isolated applications.

The process began with four seminal seed methodologies representing the foundations of analytics project
development: Knowledge Discovery in Databases (Fayyad et al., 1996a), Sample–Explore–Modify–Model–Assess
(SEMMA; SAS Institute, 1998), Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining (CRISP-DM; Chapman et al.,
2000), and Analytics Solutions Unified Method for Data Mining (ASUM-DM; IBM, 2015). A forward-citation search
was conducted in the Scopus database to retrieve all publications that cited any of these seed papers between 2010

and 2025.


Each retrieved document was screened to determine whether it proposed, formalized, or extended a methodological
framework for analytics or data-science projects. Only those documents that introduced a genuinely new methodology
or a substantive modification to an existing one were incorporated as additional seeds for the next iteration. This
selective, recursive expansion ensured controlled growth of the seed set and avoided indiscriminate inclusion of citing
documents. The process was repeated until saturation was reached—that is, when no additional unique methodologies
appeared among the newly retrieved citing records. The parameters applied in the search process are summarized in
**Table 1**, and the iterative expansion workflow is illustrated in **Fig. 1** .

It should be noted that the citation-expansion search might omit frameworks developed entirely independently of
the classical methodological lineage—that is, those not citing any of the seed papers (KDD, SEMMA, CRISP-DM,
or ASUM-DM). However, such cases appear rare. Saltz and Krasteva (2022) observed that the vast majority of recent
analytics methodologies are direct extensions or modifications of these foundational models rather than entirely novel

**3.2** **Data collection and preparation**


After the final iteration, all records were consolidated and filtered according to the following criteria:

(a) explicit citation of at least one seed or descendant methodology.
(b) proposal or modification of a structured process for executing analytics projects; and
(c) publication as a peer-reviewed journal article or conference paper.
Studies focusing solely on algorithmic innovation, software implementation, or isolated case applications without
making methodological contributions were excluded.

The iterative process initially retrieved 699 records, which yielded a final corpus of 18 methodological documents
after successive screening and consolidation. Two reviewers conducted Screening and classification independently,
and discrepancies were resolved by consensus to ensure methodological reliability. Table 1 summarizes the parameters
applied in the citation-based search, and Figure 1 visualizes the selective, iterative workflow described above.

All records retrieved through the iterative citation-based search were exported from Scopus in CSV format and
consolidated into a single dataset. Duplicate entries were identified and removed, and the remaining documents were
manually screened to ensure consistency with the inclusion criteria described in Section 3.1.

The final dataset comprised 18 methodological documents obtained through the selective citation-expansion
process illustrated in Figure 1. For each methodology, a structured extraction sheet was used to record publication
year, methodology name, domain of origin, the phases or stages defined by the source, the methodological
recommendations associated with them, distinctive features, and references to methodological or governance
standards (e.g., ISO/IEC 29110, IEEE 7000). At this stage, the terminology and organizational structure reported by
each source were preserved rather than immediately standardized into a common phase structure.

Two reviewers extracted the data independently and then reconciled them through consensus to ensure consistency
of the source-level information. The resulting records provided input for the sequential structural-reconciliation
procedure described in Section 3.3 and, after the final PRODIG8 architecture was established, for the subsequent
mapping and consolidation of methodological recommendations.


**3.3** **Data analysis**


The 18 methodological documents were analyzed sequentially through an iterative structural-reconciliation
procedure. The phases of the first document were used to establish an initial provisional structure for executing
analytics projects. The methodological functions identified in each subsequent document were then compared with
the current provisional structure. The phases proposed by each subsequent methodology were then compared with the
current structure. At each iteration, the structure was retained when the incoming phases were already represented or
modified when substantive differences were identified. Modifications involved adding phases that captured previously
unrepresented functions, renaming existing phases to accommodate broader or more precise meanings, merging
conceptually equivalent phases, or splitting phases when a methodology distinguished functions previously
represented together. This procedure was repeated sequentially across the 18 methodologies, with each resulting

structure becoming the basis for comparison with the next methodology, until the final set of eight phases constituting

Once the final eight-phase structure was established, the 18 methodologies were reviewed again, and their
methodological recommendations were mapped to the corresponding PRODIG8 phases. This second analytical stage
used the final phase structure as a common reference framework, allowing recommendations expressed through
different terminology, levels of granularity, or methodological organization to be systematically aligned. The
recommendations assigned to each phase were subsequently compared and consolidated by integrating
complementary contributions and eliminating conceptual redundancies while preserving distinctive methodological
guidance. This process produced the consolidated content of each PRODIG8 phase and maintained traceability
between the unified framework and the methodologies from which its recommendations were derived.

Two reviewers performed both analytical stages—the iterative construction of the phase structure and the
subsequent mapping and consolidation of methodological recommendations—independently. The reviewers resolved
differences in phase reconciliation, recommendation mapping, or interpretation through discussion and consensus to
ensure consistency in constructing PRODIG8.

Four transformation operations governed structural reconciliation. Addition was applied when a methodology
introduced a function not represented in the provisional structure. Renaming was applied when an existing phase
represented the same underlying function but required a broader or more precise designation to accommodate the
incoming methodological contribution. Merging was applied when phases represented separately in the provisional
and incoming structures were determined to perform conceptually equivalent functions. Splitting was applied when
an incoming methodology differentiated substantively distinct functions that were represented jointly in an existing
phase. When none of these conditions applied, the existing phase structure was retained. These operations provided
consistent decision rules for progressively reconciling heterogeneous methodological structures while preserving
substantive distinctions among their project-execution functions.


**3.4** **Scope of Quality Appraisal**


Because this study aimed to map and conceptualize methodological frameworks rather than evaluate empirical
evidence, no formal quality appraisal was conducted. This decision aligns with established guidance on scoping and
conceptual reviews, which aim to identify, synthesize, and conceptualize existing approaches rather than assess
methodological rigor (Munn et al., 2018; Peters et al., 2020). The adopted approach ensures transparency and
consistency with best practices for evidence synthesis in exploratory and theory-building research.


**4** **Results**


This section presents the results of constructing PRODIG8 from the 18 selected methodologies. The sequential
structural-reconciliation procedure progressively transformed the provisional phase structure as successive
methodologies introduced equivalent, broader, narrower, or previously unrepresented functions. After reconciling all
18 methodologies, the procedure produced a stable architecture comprising eight interdependent phases. The complete
corpus was then analyzed a second time, with each methodology's recommendations mapped to this final architecture
and consolidated within the corresponding phases. The following subsections therefore present both the resulting
phase structure and the methodological content assigned to each phase.

The resulting model, hereafter referred to as PRODIG8, comprises eight dimensions for structuring business
analytics project execution. The term “PRODIG8” summarizes Project, Data, and Governance, reflecting its
integration of project execution, data-centric rigor, and governance-oriented accountability. Rather than directly
adopting any single methodology, the eight-phase architecture reflects the cumulative result of structural
transformations identified across the 18 methodologies and the subsequent consolidation of their methodological

recommendations.


Each phase is distinctly defined, with clear conceptual and procedural boundaries that ensure consistency and
avoid overlap. The framework integrates DMME's logic by embedding its technical phases into data preparation and
deployment activities, ensuring that sensor-based data collection and engineering context are explicitly addressed
alongside governance and ethical oversight. In doing so, the unified model preserves the lineage of DMME’s three
technical phases—Technical Understanding, Technical Realization, and Technical Implementation—embedding them
within the Data Preparation and Operation & Maintenance phases to maintain traceability between industrial
engineering and business analytics contexts. Empirical demonstrations of DMME in vibration analysis and predictive
maintenance testbeds confirm the feasibility and reproducibility of its phases for sensor-based data integration and

runtime deployment in manufacturing environments. For example, Data Preparation encompasses data transformation,
integration, and quality enhancement, whereas Project Design focuses on selecting, training, and prototyping
analytical models. Also, curriculum analytics studies based on CRISP-DM have successfully implemented the unified

risks and optimize academic trajectories (Herrera Franco et al., 2025).

The sequential reconciliation also showed how the architecture of analytics-project methodologies expanded as
new methodological concerns emerged. Earlier frameworks primarily established the core analytical workflow,
whereas subsequent methodologies progressively differentiated project definition, technical and organizational
activities, evaluation, operationalization, governance, and iterative learning. A phase was retained in the evolving
structure when subsequent methodologies represented the same underlying function, while structural changes were
introduced when a methodology distinguished a function that the existing architecture could not adequately represent.

**4.1** **Project Scope Definition**


In this phase, methodologies converge on the principle that a business analytics project must always start with an
explicit and multidimensional scope definition that integrates business needs, technical feasibility, and stakeholder
alignment. The DMME framework insists that business objectives must be translated into measurable technical goals,
directly linking them to system structures, processes, and operational constraints. This ensures that ambitions such as
predictive maintenance or quality improvement are grounded in realistic parameters and technological limitations,
creating a bridge between managerial priorities and engineering contexts (Huber et al., 2019). Similarly, other
approaches emphasize the importance of beginning with a precise formulation of the research or analytical problem,
including the selection of relevant variables, dimensions, and expected contributions. By framing the analysis in
theoretical and empirical foundations, the scope gains both academic legitimacy and practical applicability (Angée
et al., 2018). MAISTRO reinforces this vision by urging organizations to align artificial intelligence initiatives with
strategic imperatives, focusing on genuine demands rather than speculative uses of technology. In this regard, the
methodology prioritizes understanding user needs, evaluating feasibility, and establishing success criteria that go
beyond traditional measures of scope, cost, and time, extending toward business impact, adoption, satisfaction, and

the incorporation of ethical and security standards. Furthermore, it emphasizes defining the team’s skills and
capabilities from the start to ensure alignment with both technical and business goals (Petrin et al., 2025).

CRISP-DM has historically positioned business understanding as the cornerstone of analytics projects,
recommending that project objectives and requirements be clarified in business terms and then translated into technical
data mining formulations. In this phase, the methodology suggests assessing the situation in terms of resources,
assumptions, risks, costs, and benefits, followed by defining success criteria that capture both business and analytical
perspectives. The outcome is a preliminary project plan that aligns stakeholders’ expectations and sets the foundation
for subsequent steps (Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025; Plotnikova et al., 2020; Wirth & Hipp, 2000). Complementary
approaches emphasize the importance of contextual analysis, in which project motivation and stakeholder needs are
clarified and feasibility is evaluated. For example, the use of project charters, detailed timelines, and explicit project
plans ensures that assumptions, constraints, and intended steps are visible to all parties involved (Bemthuis et al.,
2025). Agile-inspired frameworks introduce practices such as Sprint 0, during which objectives, roles, and backlogs
are defined through user stories, creativity workshops, and integration sessions. These practices foster early
collaboration and adaptability, helping teams refine priorities as understanding evolves (Hamdani et al., 2024; Hayat
Suhendar & Widyani, 2023).

The DataPro methodology formalizes the duality between business and technical perspectives by recommending
that stakeholders articulate strategic goals and expected outcomes. In contrast, technical teams translate these into
precise data-science formulations of inputs, outputs, constraints, and quantitative evaluation criteria. This dual
approach reduces ambiguity, enhances communication, and ensures that both dimensions progress in parallel rather
than in isolation (Ma et al., 2025). In institutional or academic contexts, scope definition often means aligning project
objectives with key performance indicators relevant to education, such as retention rates, graduation rates, and
satisfaction levels. Visualization tools like Bizagi BPM may be used to clarify dependencies and bottlenecks,
embedding institutional strategy within the scope phase (Azeroual et al., 2025; Herrera Franco et al., 2025). Scientific
methodologies, on the other hand, highlight that the scope must address the gap between large-scale data collection
and the limited capacity for human analysis. For this reason, they propose framing KDD as an interface that automates
large-scale data-handling tasks, enabling scientists to devote their efforts to hypothesis generation and theoretical
interpretation. This implies that the scope must explicitly include provisions for scalable infrastructure to process highdimensional data environments (Fayyad et al., 2000).

Enhanced versions of CRISP-DM advance this discussion by extending the scope definition beyond purely
technical or managerial objectives. They include considerations such as value potential across economic, social, and
environmental dimensions; human–machine interaction factors, such as ethics, trust, and engagement; and an explicit
evaluation of whether AI is the appropriate solution. In addition, they recommend designing early conceptual life
cycles of the AI solution, even if incomplete, to identify hidden maintenance costs and anticipate structural design
flaws that could undermine long-term value (Bokrantz et al., 2024). Methodologies with a research orientation argue
that scope definition must also ensure that problem identification is justified by business or societal needs, that
problems are conceptualized, that constraints are clarified, and that objectives are aligned with both quantitative and
qualitative measures of success. This iterative refinement between objectives and data insights avoids ex-post
justification and reinforces relevance and feasibility (Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025).

In all methodologies, scope definition is not treated as a static, one-time event but rather as an iterative, evolving
process. Objectives may be refined based on new insights or changing stakeholder needs, ensuring that analytical
initiatives remain relevant and actionable over time (Fois et al., 2020; Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023). This
dynamic view underscores the importance of continuous engagement, transparent communication, and adaptive
planning tools that sustain alignment between project direction and organizational strategy. Thus, across DMME,
CRISP-DM, MAISTRO, DataPro, and other frameworks, the unified guideline for this phase requires that analytics
projects establish, from the outset, a shared foundation where business priorities, technical feasibility, ethical foresight,
and stakeholder alignment converge. Only through this comprehensive scoping process can later stages of data
understanding, preparation, modeling, and deployment achieve their intended impact without losing sight of broader
organizational objectives. In sum, scope definition emerges as a multidimensional phase that integrates strategic intent,
operational reality, scientific rigor, and ethical considerations, creating the structured basis upon which the
sustainability and success of business analytics projects depend.


**4.2** **Data understanding**


In this phase, data understanding is positioned as a central activity that bridges project scope and data preparation,
ensuring that available data meaningfully support the project’s analytical objectives. It integrates business intent,

technical feasibility, and domain knowledge, serving as the foundation for all subsequent stages of an analytics project.
It begins with the systematic collection of raw data from diverse sources, whether originating in organizational
systems, sensors, logs, social platforms, or academic repositories, and proceeds to their description, exploration, and
validation (Azeroual et al., 2025; Bemthuis et al., 2025; Bhardwaj, 2020; Hamdani et al., 2024; Huber et al., 2019;
Wirth & Hipp, 2000). The exploration process requires generating descriptive reports that characterize distributions,
anomalies, correlations, and structures in the data while documenting initial hypotheses about hidden information or
relevant variables that could influence outcomes (Angée et al., 2018; Azeroual et al., 2025; Montoya-Murillo et al.,
2025). Structured frameworks such as the 6Vs model are frequently cited for assessing data quality across dimensions
of completeness, accuracy, timeliness, consistency, and representativeness, helping anticipate challenges and confirm
dataset adequacy (Ma et al., 2025; Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). By combining
empirical findings with theoretical models or domain knowledge, practitioners ensure that the exploratory insights not
only reveal statistical properties but also contextual meaning, thereby strengthening the alignment between available
data and business-driven goals (Angée et al., 2018; Bokrantz et al., 2024; Herrera Franco et al., 2025; Huber et al.,
2019; Petrin et al., 2025).

A critical recommendation is that data understanding is not a linear task but rather an iterative, cyclical process
that requires ongoing refinement in response to project needs and evaluation feedback (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Petrin
et al., 2025; Plotnikova et al., 2020; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). For instance, detecting missing values, duplicate
records, or inconsistencies may prompt additional data acquisition or preprocessing before modeling, ensuring that
data adequacy evolves in tandem with analytical objectives (Bemthuis et al., 2025; Bhardwaj, 2020; Fayyad et al.,
2000; Ma et al., 2025; Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025). This adaptability underscores that data understanding evolves
alongside project scope, with each cycle of exploration offering the opportunity to refine objectives, adjust hypotheses,
and improve data adequacy (Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023; Petrin et al., 2025; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024;
Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Moreover, methodologies consistently highlight the importance of linking business needs
with data exploration by ensuring that identified variables, subsets, and features remain relevant to operational
decisions, strategic goals, or scientific inquiry (Hamdani et al., 2024; Herrera Franco et al., 2025; Petrin et al., 2025;
Wirth & Hipp, 2000). This interplay not only validates assumptions but also helps anticipate risks, ensuring that the
project maintains focus on its intended outcomes even as technical details evolve.

Domain expertise plays a decisive role during this phase, as practitioners must interpret raw measurements, signals,
or categorical indicators within their operational or scientific context (Fayyad et al., 2000; Hamdani et al., 2024;
Herrera Franco et al., 2025; Huber et al., 2019). Engineering knowledge, for example, enables the transformation of
vibration signals into features that reveal component deterioration, while familiarity with academic processes enables
the identification of dropout risk factors in educational data (Herrera Franco et al., 2025; Huber et al., 2019). Such
expertise is equally essential when handling heterogeneous datasets—ranging from time-series logs and text streams
to genetic sequences and images—where the challenge lies in extracting attributes that meaningfully represent
underlying phenomena rather than noise (Bemthuis et al., 2025; Bhardwaj, 2020; Bokrantz et al., 2024; Fayyad et al.,
2000). Feature extraction and representation, therefore, become core practices in this stage, ensuring that subsequent
modeling is built on relevant and interpretable variables (Fayyad et al., 2000; Huber et al., 2019). These activities not
only facilitate technical readiness but also foster a shared understanding across interdisciplinary teams, particularly in
projects that require bridging perspectives from different fields (Angée et al., 2018; Bokrantz et al., 2024; Hamdani
et al., 2024; Herrera Franco et al., 2025).

At the organizational level, data understanding also requires attention to compliance, access, and governance,
given that datasets are often generated across distributed systems and may be subject to regulatory constraints or userspecific contexts (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Hamdani et al., 2024; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Addressing these factors
ensures that exploration remains not only technically rigorous but also ethically and legally sound, aligned with
institutional governance requirements (Hamdani et al., 2024; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Practical outcomes of this
phase include formal documentation such as data description reports, exploration reports, and quality assessment
reports, which serve as artifacts to support transparency and reproducibility (Fois et al., 2020; Wirth & Hipp, 2000).
Additionally, exploratory outputs such as statistical summaries, visualizations, and descriptive dashboards serve as
intermediate deliverables that facilitate communication with stakeholders, enabling iterative validation of assumptions
and early alignment between technical findings and managerial expectations (Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023;
Herrera Franco et al., 2025; Wirth & Hipp, 2000; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025).

Ultimately, the phase of data understanding serves as both a diagnostic and constructive step: diagnostic, in that it
identifies anomalies, limitations, and risks that could undermine modeling, and constructive, in that it generates
preliminary hypotheses, validates feasibility, and defines the operational meaning of data (Angée et al., 2018; Huber
et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2025). By establishing a continuous cycle of exploration, evaluation, and refinement, this phase

**4.3** **Data preparation**


In this phase, the focus is on systematically transforming raw, heterogeneous, and often noisy data into structured,
high-quality, and analyzable datasets that can support reliable modeling and robust decision-making. Serving as a
bridge between exploratory understanding and analytical modeling, this phase consolidates exploratory insights from
data understanding and operationalizes them through systematic acquisition, cleaning, integration, and feature
engineering. It ensures that preparatory work remains aligned with business intent, technical feasibility, and domain
knowledge. The process begins with rigorous data acquisition, where information is collected from diverse internal
and external sources, including sensors, logs, transactional records, or textual content, and structured using
standardized designs that ensure representativeness and analytical validity (Huber et al., 2019). From the outset,
explicit documentation of procedures, data quality, limitations, and potential errors is emphasized as a mechanism to
secure transparency and accountability throughout the preparation cycle (Huber et al., 2019). Once acquired, data
undergoes cleaning and depuration processes that include managing missing values, correcting inconsistencies,
detecting and addressing outliers, anonymizing sensitive attributes, and scrubbing noisy records, such as unfiltered
social media content. These operations are indispensable for preventing distortions that could compromise subsequent
analyses (Angée et al., 2018; Bhardwaj, 2020; Herrera Franco et al., 2025). Textual and unstructured data require
specific transformations, such as tokenization, stemming, lemmatization, stopword filtering, or balancing textual
classes to ensure comparability and avoid bias. In contrast, numerical datasets require normalization, rescaling, or
redundancy reduction (Azeroual et al., 2025). Across both structured and unstructured domains, feature engineering
plays a central role: practitioners construct derived variables, select indicators relevant to business and domain
objectives, and encode meaningful representations that capture latent patterns while discarding noise (Bokrantz et al.,
2024; Ma et al., 2025; Wirth & Hipp, 2000; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025).

The methodology consistently portrays data preparation as iterative, adaptive, and highly path-dependent. Tasks
such as cleaning, integration, transformation, and feature construction are rarely linear; instead, they are repeated
multiple times as evaluation feedback or sprint-based development identifies inadequacies that must be addressed
(Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025; Petrin et al., 2025; Plotnikova et al., 2020; Wirth & Hipp, 2000). Agile and hybrid
frameworks reinforce this adaptability by aligning preparation cycles with sprint iterations and parallel modeling
activities, preventing bottlenecks and ensuring that data processing evolves alongside project needs (Hamdani et al.,
2024; Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023). Iteration is not only technical but also conceptual: initial design decisions
regarding feature labeling, sampling strategies, or integration approaches shape the scope of subsequent preparation
activities. They may amplify downstream complexity if handled inadequately (Bokrantz et al., 2024). This path
dependency makes early documentation, rationalization of choices, and explicit evaluation of performance metrics
crucial for preserving analytical rigor and operational feasibility (Bokrantz et al., 2024). The literature further
highlights the importance of dimensionality reduction, whether through projection, segmentation, or partitioning, to
generate compact and computationally manageable datasets without sacrificing representational power (Angée et al.,
2018; Fayyad et al., 2000; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Concrete applications, such as transforming astronomical
images into object descriptors, segmenting DNA sequences into functional regions, or reformatting raw simulation
logs into process-mining event logs, illustrate how reduction and structuring preserve relevant information while
enhancing interpretability (Bemthuis et al., 2025; Fayyad et al., 2000).

Integration emerges as another recurring principle, ensuring that data from multiple and often inconsistent sources
is harmonized, reconciled, and consolidated into unified structures. Tasks include merging datasets, aligning formats,
standardizing scales, and ensuring comparability across variables and sites (Hamdani et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025;
Wirth & Hipp, 2000; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025).

This integration not only addresses technical incompatibilities but also mitigates risks of fragmentation and
duplication, which would otherwise reduce trust in the results. Secure environments and automated pipelines are
recommended enablers for this integration, enabling scalability, efficiency, and consistency in projects that involve
continuous or real-time inputs (Fois et al., 2020; Herrera Franco et al., 2025).

The guidelines consistently underline that the preparation phase is resource-intensive and requires robust
infrastructure, particularly when dealing with large-scale or high-dimensional datasets (Bemthuis et al., 2025; Herrera
Franco et al., 2025; Petrin et al., 2025). Technical environments that ensure security, scalability, and systematic

processing are considered prerequisites for not only high-quality data but also compliance with organizational and
regulatory standards (Fois et al., 2020; Herrera Franco et al., 2025).

Another critical dimension of preparation is the balance between automation and human expertise. Automated
reduction, cataloging, or imputation procedures accelerate preprocessing and improve scalability, yet domain expertise
remains essential for feature selection, labeling, and interpretation of context-dependent signals (Bokrantz et al., 2024;
Fayyad et al., 2000; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Domain knowledge ensures that derived variables are not only
statistically meaningful but also aligned with operational and business objectives, strengthening the interpretability
and applicability of analytical outcomes (Bokrantz et al., 2024). This dual reliance underscores that preparation is both
a technical and an organizational activity, requiring collaboration among data scientists, engineers, and domain
specialists (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025). Recommendations stress the need for continuous feedback loops:
preparation decisions must be validated against modeling requirements and business constraints, with failures or
inefficiencies in later phases feeding back into refinement cycles (Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023; Petrin et al.,
2025; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Such bidirectional flows guarantee that datasets are not only technically sound but
also practically relevant, enabling the models trained on them to generate actionable insights (Hayat Suhendar &
Widyani, 2023; Ma et al., 2025).

Deliverables of this phase extend beyond the dataset itself. The literature recommends producing detailed
preparation reports documenting acquisition, cleaning activities, feature construction, and integration logic. These
artifacts reinforce transparency, reproducibility, and organizational learning while providing a foundation for auditing,
compliance, and governance processes. Structured pipelines and technical environments further support repeatability,
allowing future updates or dataset extensions without re-engineering the entire preparation workflow (Fois et al., 2020;
Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024; Wirth & Hipp, 2000). Far from being a static stage, data preparation is conceived as a
living, evolving process that spans the entire project lifecycle, tightly linked to both initial scoping and downstream
modeling (Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023; Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025; Wirth & Hipp, 2000). Properly executed,
it delivers datasets that are representative, consistent, interpretable, and computationally efficient, while safeguarding
security, scalability, and compliance requirements (Fois et al., 2020; Herrera Franco et al., 2025).

Overall, this phase emerges as the cornerstone of business analytics projects, characterized by its iterative nature,
reliance on domain expertise, and strong emphasis on technical rigor. It transforms raw, fragmented, and
heterogeneous inputs into structured, analyzable datasets that support robust modeling and trustworthy decisionmaking. Minor errors or omissions at this stage are shown to propagate through the lifecycle, amplifying operational
complexity and undermining analytical outcomes (Bokrantz et al., 2024). Conversely, well-designed preparation
processes create high-quality datasets that serve as key enablers of effective modeling and reliable insights (Wirth &
Hipp, 2000; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). For this reason, the literature uniformly underscores that preparation must be
executed with systematic discipline, iterative refinement, and organizational commitment, ensuring that the datasets
generated are not only valid for analytical purposes but also aligned with the strategic objectives and practical
constraints of the business context (Fois et al., 2020; Hamdani et al., 2024; Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023; Petrin
et al., 2025).


**4.4** **Project Design**


In this phase, the emphasis is on modeling as the central development activity, with conceptual objectives
translated into tangible, testable, and progressively refined analytical artifacts. Building on the structured datasets
generated in previous phases, modeling is both an analytical and creative process that guides the transformation of
theoretical objectives into practical solutions, such as predictive models, classification systems, and analytical
frameworks. The process begins with the design of test structures that guide systematic assessment, enabling the
generation of multiple candidate models whose assumptions, parameter settings, and required data formats must be
critically evaluated (Fois et al., 2020; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). A bidirectional interaction with data understanding
and preparation is consistently emphasized, as insights derived from modeling often reveal inconsistencies,
limitations, or opportunities for data enhancement (Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023; Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025;
Wirth & Hipp, 2000). This cyclical design ensures that refinements are not only technical adjustments but also
contribute to the coherence and quality of the overall solution. Iteration across sprints and agile cycles plays a crucial
role, as the process is inherently adaptive and oriented toward incremental improvement, with prototypes and partial
deliverables serving as tangible checkpoints of progress (Hamdani et al., 2024; Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023;
Petrin et al., 2025).

The methodologies converge in underscoring the need to apply multiple statistical, computational, and machine
learning techniques, along with parameter calibration, dimensionality reduction, and feature engineering, to enhance

the use of both structured and unstructured features that expand the models’ explanatory and predictive capacity
(Azeroual et al., 2025; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Modeling is portrayed not as a linear process but as a recursive and
adaptive one, often requiring revisiting previous steps when discrepancies arise between empirical behavior and
theoretical expectations. This adaptability extends beyond technical challenges to address organizational and
operational constraints (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Huber et al., 2019).

A distinctive guideline across methodologies is the incorporation of multidisciplinary collaboration and domain

the use of process mining, expert validation, and domain-driven interpretation emphasizes that modeling must account
for complexity, manage outliers, and balance behavioral coverage with performance indicators (Bemthuis et al., 2025;
Herrera Franco et al., 2025). Such integration guarantees that the resulting artifacts are not only accurate but also

as an engineering discipline capable of delivering solutions that integrate into operational pipelines, remain adaptable
to new information vectors, and sustain performance over time (Huber et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2025). The iterative
development of minimal viable models and prototypes demonstrates the capacity to respond quickly to misalignments,
to refine dashboards or system outputs, and to sustain user-centered design that prioritizes responsiveness and

The iterative experimentation cycle also emphasizes balancing innovation with feasibility. Creative and theoretical

scalability, interpretability, and practicality, ensuring that final artifacts are scientifically valid while operationally
sustainable (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Huber et al., 2019; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Deliverables from this phase extend
beyond technical outputs to include structured documentation, refinement logs, and knowledge-transfer guidelines
that promote traceability, transparency, and institutional learning (Fois et al., 2020; Wirth & Hipp, 2000).

Ultimately, the methodological synthesis highlights modeling as a recursive, collaborative, and adaptive process

**4.5** **Model Evaluation**


In this phase, model evaluation is conceived as a pivotal quality and alignment checkpoint that bridges model
development and deployment, ensuring developed models, insights, and artifacts are not only technically sound but
also relevant, actionable, and aligned with organizational objectives. Evaluation is understood as a rigorous, multilayered, and iterative process embedded throughout the project lifecycle rather than limited to its conclusion. It begins
by assessing model performance against predefined technical and business success criteria, including accuracy,
precision, recall, latency, operational efficiency, adoption rates, and user satisfaction (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Ma et al.,
2025; Petrin et al., 2025; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). Models are validated using new or unseen data, alternative
samples, or resampling techniques to ensure robustness and generalizability, thereby mitigating risks of overfitting or
premature deployment (Angée et al., 2018; Azeroual et al., 2025; Huber et al., 2019; Plotnikova et al., 2020). Beyond
technical quality, this phase emphasizes ensuring that outcomes remain consistent with business objectives and

to generate measurable business value (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2025; Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025; SalazarSalazar et al., 2024; Wirth & Hipp, 2000). Iterative feedback loops are integral to the evaluation process: results may
necessitate revisiting earlier phases such as data preparation, modeling, or even objective definition, thereby enabling
continuous refinement and adaptation (Bemthuis et al., 2025; Hamdani et al., 2024; Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023;
Petrin et al., 2025; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025).

Evaluation further incorporates systematic comparisons against benchmarks, diagnostic measures, and domainspecific ground truths. These include tests across languages in text analytics, validation against scientific observations

methodological rigor and domain relevance (Bhardwaj, 2020; Fayyad et al., 2000; Herrera Franco et al., 2025). Such

where scientific credibility demands confidence levels exceeding 90% (Fayyad et al., 2000). Likewise, the evaluation
process integrates expert judgment and stakeholder validation, where results are scrutinized for plausibility, reliability,
and practical applicability before broader adoption (Bemthuis et al., 2025; Fois et al., 2020; Hayat Suhendar &
Widyani, 2023; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). This dual perspective—technical soundness and domain or business
relevance—ensures that models progress only if they satisfy both dimensions.

A distinguishing feature of this phase is its iterative and cyclical nature. Evaluation checkpoints are integrated into
project governance activities—such as sprint reviews, stakeholder meetings, and prototype testing—to identify
misalignments early, sustain engagement, and guide incremental improvement (Hamdani et al., 2024; Hayat Suhendar
& Widyani, 2023). These checkpoints allow early identification of misalignments, reinforce stakeholder engagement,
and promote the incremental evolution of models and tools toward business impact (Hamdani et al., 2024; Hayat
Suhendar & Widyani, 2023). Furthermore, evaluation explicitly includes trade-off analysis, balancing criteria such as
accuracy versus interpretability, computational cost versus scalability, or predictive performance versus usability,
ensuring that chosen solutions remain both robust and feasible in production contexts (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Zolbanin
& Aubert, 2025). Complementary validation processes—including user acceptance testing and system verification—
provide safeguards that ensure operational readiness and compliance with institutional or regulatory standards before
deployment (Fois et al., 2020).

The methodological novelty of this phase lies in combining quantitative validation with qualitative and
organizational assessments. Metrics such as AUC, F1-score, Precision-Recall, and Matthews Correlation Coefficient
provide statistical rigor (Azeroual et al., 2025; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025), while structured reviews, expert
assessments, and stakeholder acceptance confirm interpretability, trust, and alignment with organizational goals
(Bemthuis et al., 2025; Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). Deliverables emerging from
this phase include evaluation reports, documentation of process reviews, and lists of possible subsequent actions,
which function as decision points for advancing or refining models (Fois et al., 2020; Wirth & Hipp, 2000). Overall,
evaluation is framed not as a terminal checkpoint but as a continuous mechanism of quality assurance, business
alignment, and organizational learning that bridges analytical development with deployment readiness. By
systematically integrating technical metrics, business indicators, domain-specific benchmarks, and iterative
stakeholder feedback, this phase ensures that business analytics projects deliver statistically valid outcomes,
operationally robust, and strategically valuable (Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024; Wirth &
Hipp, 2000; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025).


**4.6** **Governance and Ethics**


In this phase, governance and ethics operate as transversal and foundational dimensions that permeate the entire
analytics project lifecycle, operating in parallel with the technical evaluation of models to ensure that results are not
only accurate but also aligned with organizational, legal, and cultural values. Rather than a discrete or final step,
governance functions as a continuous safeguard that ensures analytical outputs remain technically sound while
consistent with legal, organizational, and societal standards. This cross-cutting integration reinforces that governance
is inseparable from analytics execution, guiding decisions from project initiation to long-term maintenance. This
dedicated phase ensures that analytics projects adhere to evolving AI governance standards by institutionalizing
traceability, transparency, and accountability throughout the lifecycle—principles emphasized in the Ethics
Guidelines for Trustworthy AI issued by the European Commission’s High-Level Expert Group on AI (2019).
Embedding these principles into every phase of analytics practice transforms governance and ethics into proactive
design components that prevent bias, opacity, and noncompliance from arising as afterthoughts, fostering responsible
innovation and public trust. This inclusion aligns with international AI governance standards emphasizing traceability

Artificial Intelligence, 2019). Evaluation requires that models are validated against both business and technical
objectives, using robust procedures to guarantee predictive reliability and actionable insights under real operational
conditions (Huber et al., 2019). This validation is complemented by governance structures that provide role clarity,
stakeholder accountability, and transparent documentation of assumptions and processes, thereby reinforcing trust in
the outputs (Hamdani et al., 2024; Herrera Franco et al., 2025). Governance is not conceived as a separate or latestage activity but rather as a cross-functional integration of experts, continuous feedback loops, and transparent

foster a culture of organizational learning and to integrate ethical considerations into daily decision-making, rather
than treating them as isolated verifications (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Hamdani et al., 2024). This orientation reframes
governance and ethics from compliance requirements into strategic enablers of sustainable innovation, directly
influencing the usability, scalability, and credibility of analytic solutions. The integration of monitoring mechanisms,
transparency checks, and user feedback into the evaluation process ensures that models remain resilient to changes in
data quality dimensions, such as completeness, plausibility, timeliness, and consistency (Azeroual et al., 2025). At the

(Azeroual et al., 2025). While some methodologies mention ethics only briefly in the data understanding phase,
without structured recommendations (Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025), the convergence of CRISP-DM extensions,
MAISTRO, and other frameworks demonstrates a clear shift toward embedding governance and ethics as ongoing,
systemic practices, inseparable from technical validation (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Petrin et al., 2025). Ultimately, this
phase reframes evaluation as a holistic assurance process—where transparency, compliance, accountability, and
fairness are continuously monitored and operationalized across all stages of analytics execution—thereby aligning
analytical systems with both strategic business objectives and the ethical responsibilities of the organization (Azeroual
et al., 2025; Bokrantz et al., 2024; Hamdani et al., 2024; Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025).


**4.7** **Operation and Maintenance**


In this phase, operation and maintenance are reconceptualized so that deployment is understood not as a final step
but as the beginning of an ongoing operational lifecycle in which sustainability, adaptability, and monitoring are
central to ensuring that analytical solutions continue to deliver value. Frameworks such as DMME emphasize the
importance of connecting models to runtime-capable infrastructures and providing long-term resilience in
environments with continuous data flows (Huber et al., 2019). Similarly, MAISTRO advances a structured perspective
on operationalization, emphasizing version management, retraining cycles, and constant monitoring as mechanisms
of adaptability and sustained performance (Petrin et al., 2025). Extensions of CRISP-DM formalize these principles
by including explicit tracking, updating, and sustaining tasks, and by recommending automation, retraining policies,
and human-in-the-loop mechanisms to maintain trust and relevance over time (Bokrantz et al., 2024). The convergence
of these recommendations indicates that successful deployment requires integrating predictive and prescriptive
outputs directly into organizational decision workflows, thereby embedding analytical tools into daily operations
rather than treating them as stand-alone artifacts (Azeroual et al., 2025; Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025).

Monitoring emerges as the guiding principle of the phase, with methodologies such as TDSP and DDSL
underscoring dashboards, system health indicators, and periodic supervision as core deliverables (Salazar-Salazar
et al., 2024). These tools ensure that deployed solutions remain aligned with strategic objectives, provide real-time
visibility, and reduce reliance on static reports (Hamdani et al., 2024). In both industrial and institutional contexts,
dashboards and predictive alerts are used to continuously track indicators—ranging from production alignment to

academic performance—facilitating proactive interventions and reducing delays in corrective action (Herrera Franco
et al., 2025). The operational challenge is not only technological but organizational, as methodologies acknowledge
the presence of political, cultural, and economic complexities that condition the adoption and sustainability of
deployed solutions (Bemthuis et al., 2025). This is particularly relevant in data-intensive domains that require
scalability and computational efficiency, where adaptive infrastructures and high-performance environments ensure
analytical continuity amid growing data volumes (Fayyad et al., 2000).

Sustainability in deployment thus implies a dual responsibility: ensuring the robustness of technical infrastructures
and aligning ongoing operation with institutional governance. Recommendations call for sustained retraining, drift
detection, and incremental model refinement to ensure predictive tools remain accurate as environments shift
(Bokrantz et al., 2024). These processes extend to managing production systems, overseeing infrastructure,
documenting procedures, and optimizing resources, while also ensuring security and defect management (Fois et al.,
2020). Governance emerges as a transversal layer, requiring policies for time-based or condition-based maintenance
of data pipelines and deployed models (Bokrantz et al., 2024), complemented by help-desk administration and user
support communities that reinforce adoption and trust (Fois et al., 2020). Through this dual perspective—technical
and institutional—operation becomes a living, governance-driven process of sustainability that embeds analytics into
the organization’s daily operational culture. Far from a purely technical exercise, deployment involves integrating
analytical insights into existing institutional routines, whether through supply-chain coordination (Hamdani et al.,
2024), academic management processes (Herrera Franco et al., 2025), or research workflows connected to evolving
data sources (Azeroual et al., 2025).

At the same time, deployment cannot be detached from the cycle of evaluation and feedback. Case experiences
underscore the importance of iterative validation, in which monitoring not only tracks technical performance but also
verifies the benefits delivered, thereby informing adjustments in subsequent projects (Wirth & Hipp, 2000; Zolbanin
& Aubert, 2025). This feedback-driven continuity ensures that operation and evaluation remain tightly connected,
promoting refinement whenever misalignments between model outputs and organizational needs arise. This iterative
dimension emphasizes that operationalization must remain open to revisiting earlier project phases when models fail
to meet business or domain criteria (Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025). Maintenance is therefore framed as both a corrective
and generative activity, enabling continuous learning and the evolution of practices across projects. Where
methodologies fail to provide explicit post-deployment recommendations—as seen in some research-oriented
approaches (Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023)—this absence underscores the growing need for systematic
operational guidance that extends beyond initial implementation into the long-term management of AI systems.

Ultimately, this phase represents a methodological consolidation of deployment and operation as inseparable
components of business analytics projects. It integrates infrastructure design (Huber et al., 2019), structured
operational practices (Petrin et al., 2025), monitoring plans (Wirth & Hipp, 2000), governance and complexity
awareness (Bemthuis et al., 2025), and dashboards for real-time supervision (Hamdani et al., 2024; Herrera Franco
et al., 2025; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). It also incorporates domain-specific sustainability concerns such as
scalability in data-intensive contexts (Fayyad et al., 2000), institutional embedding of predictive outputs (Azeroual
et al., 2025; Montoya-Murillo et al., 2025), and explicit maintenance policies for drift detection and retraining
(Bokrantz et al., 2024; Fois et al., 2020). Taken together, these practices define operation and maintenance as dynamic,
adaptive, and governance-oriented processes that sustain analytical reliability, organizational alignment, and long
term business value.


**4.8** **Continuous Improvement**


In this phase, continuous improvement serves as the sustaining engine of the analytics lifecycle, conceived as a
cyclical, adaptive mechanism that extends across the entire lifecycle of business analytics projects, ensuring that both
methodological processes and analytical solutions remain relevant and effective in dynamic organizational
environments. Far from representing closure, this phase embodies the iterative and renewal-oriented spirit of modern
analytics practice, where learning and feedback drive the long-term evolution of analytical systems. This phase aligns
with emerging MLOps (Machine Learning Operations) practices that emphasize continuous model monitoring,
automated retraining, and pipeline updates to sustain solution performance and reliability in production environments.
Integrating these practices reinforces the governance-aware perspective of the unified framework, connecting
methodological improvement with the operational discipline required for scalable and trustworthy analytics systems.
This alignment mirrors the iterative principles of MLOps, which promote retraining, deployment supervision, and
adaptive scalability to sustain analytical value. While some methodologies, such as DMME, limit their perspective to
iterative refinement during design and evaluation without addressing long-term feedback loops (Huber et al., 2019),

others explicitly embed improvement as a structural principle. CRISP-DM, for instance, positions learning as cyclical,
where lessons learned trigger new, more focused business questions, documentation is treated as a “living” asset, and
iterations are explicitly planned to incorporate accumulated experience (Bokrantz et al., 2024; Plotnikova et al., 2020;
Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024; Wirth & Hipp, 2000). Similarly, frameworks inspired by agile approaches highlight the
roles of sprint-based refinement, retrospectives, and feedback loops in reinforcing adaptation, enhancing team skills,
and reducing rework (Hamdani et al., 2024; Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023). In the unified framework, this iterative

The methodological emphasis is on embedding feedback loops between phases to continuously refine data, models,
and evaluations. Some approaches recommend revisiting earlier phases whenever limitations are detected, iteratively
refining analytical strategies, or integrating new data sources to improve validity and generalizability (Angée et al.,
2018; Azeroual et al., 2025; Bemthuis et al., 2025). Others point to practical examples, such as refining sentiment
analysis by repeatedly collecting and cleaning real-time data, or retraining models with larger, more diverse samples
to improve robustness (Bhardwaj, 2020). Process mining examples highlight the role of repeated checks for validity,
expansion to additional indicators, and involvement of broader expert panels, illustrating that iterative face-validity
exercises are essential to sustain rigor (Bemthuis et al., 2025). Across domains, methodologies stress that improvement
is not restricted to single problem instances but requires generalization, guided emergence, and adaptation to evolving
datasets, technologies, and organizational practices (Azeroual et al., 2025; Fayyad et al., 2000; Zolbanin & Aubert,
2025). Through these mechanisms, continuous improvement operates as both a corrective and generative process,
enhancing robustness while fostering institutional learning.

Continuous improvement is also tied to the adaptability and extensibility of systems. Methodologies highlight the
importance of dynamically adjusting predictive models and strategies as conditions evolve, recommending replication
across contexts, incorporating demographic or socioeconomic variables, and exploring new modalities to sustain
relevance (Herrera Franco et al., 2025). Frameworks like MAISTRO emphasize recurring loops across phases and
underscore continuous learning not only for models but also for teams, promoting adaptation to new technologies and
practices (Petrin et al., 2025). In more technical domains, extensible environments such as SKICAT or JARtool
illustrate how methodologies evolve by expanding analytical capabilities, whether through supervised-tounsupervised extensions or through adaptation to novel image or sequence data (Fayyad et al., 2000). This
extensibility orientation ensures long-term sustainability, enabling methodologies to evolve alongside advances in
machine learning, data availability, and institutional needs (Azeroual et al., 2025).

Another recurring recommendation is to formalize reflection and learning as an explicit mechanism for
improvement. Some methodologies encourage systematic reviews of results, given their capacity to generate new
research questions or business insights (Angée et al., 2018). Others incorporate structured reflections through
retrospectives or dedicated phases to document lessons learned, conduct internal reviews, and institutionalize design
principles that transcend individual projects (Fois et al., 2020; Hayat Suhendar & Widyani, 2023; Zolbanin & Aubert,
2025). In this way, reflection serves both operational and epistemic functions—advancing methodological rigor while
enabling theoretical and organizational learning (Zolbanin & Aubert, 2025).

The overall synthesis highlights that continuous improvement requires a dual orientation: on one hand, operational
refinement through iterative optimization of data, models, and processes; and on the other, organizational and
methodological evolution through documentation, reflection, and adaptation. Thus, improvement is achieved by
revisiting prior results, adapting methods to new data and risks, and integrating user feedback and contextual insights
into subsequent iterations (Azeroual et al., 2025; Bokrantz et al., 2024; Salazar-Salazar et al., 2024). By embedding
feedback as a permanent structural feature, continuous improvement ensures that analytics projects accumulate
resilience, accuracy, and institutional knowledge—fostering sustained innovation and adaptive capacity in volatile,

data-intensive environments.

across this lifecycle; and Continuous Improvement connects project results, operational experience, and

**5** **Discussion**


Building on the comparative synthesis presented in Section 4, this discussion interprets the broader implications
of the PRODIG8 framework for advancing theory, practice, and governance in analytics project management. The
model’s contribution lies not only in consolidating methodological content across existing frameworks, but also in
differentiating three complementary functions required for analytics-project execution: a core execution lifecycle,
transversal governance and ethical control, and adaptive feedback through continuous improvement. This distinction
more explicitly represents how technical execution, organizational accountability, and learning interact within the
same methodological architecture. Accordingly, PRODIG8 extends conventional lifecycle models such as CRISPDM, ASUM-DM, and TDSP by integrating execution, control, and adaptation as distinct but interdependent
components of analytics-project management.

Unlike prior frameworks that focus on specific organizational or disciplinary settings—such as the conceptual HR
analytics model proposed by Gurusinghe et al. (2021)—the unified framework developed here bridges multiple
domains and offers methodological guidance applicable across sectors. This broader orientation positions the study as
a higher-level generalization of domain-specific approaches, consistent with the cross-disciplinary expectations of
contemporary analytics research.

The structural differentiation of PRODIG8 into execution, control, and adaptation extends the theoretical
contribution beyond methodological consolidation. From a standardization perspective, the six core execution
dimensions establish a common process architecture that reduces procedural variability while preserving
methodological interoperability across organizational contexts. From a governance perspective, treating Governance
and Ethics as a transversal control dimension recognizes accountability, transparency, risk management, and ethical
oversight as continuous requirements rather than activities confined to a particular stage. From a systems-integration
perspective, Continuous Improvement provides the feedback mechanism through which execution outcomes,
operational experience, and changing conditions can modify activities throughout the lifecycle. PRODIG8 therefore
combines process standardization with transversal control and adaptive feedback within a single architecture,
providing a theoretical explanation for how analytics methodologies can remain structured without becoming rigid.

The findings reveal a progressive methodological evolution from analytics methodologies centered primarily on
the technical execution workflow toward broader socio-technical architectures incorporating governance,
operationalization, and continuous learning. Early approaches such as KDD, SEMMA, and CRISP-DM established
the core logic for transforming business or analytical objectives and data into evaluated analytical outputs. Subsequent
methodologies progressively extended this logic by introducing stronger project-management practices, operational
and maintenance requirements, governance and ethical mechanisms, and explicit feedback and learning processes.
PRODIG8 captures this cumulative evolution by separating these methodological contributions according to their
functional role: execution dimensions structure the development and operationalization of the analytical solution,
Governance and Ethics continuously control how these activities are performed, and Continuous Improvement enables
the architecture to respond to evaluation results, operational experience, and changing conditions. PRODIG8 therefore
contributes not simply by adding activities absent from earlier methodologies, but by integrating methodological
advances accumulated across successive generations into a differentiated execution–control–adaptation architecture.
In contrast, more recent frameworks—such as DMME, ASUM-DM, TDSP, MAISTRO, and DataPro—incorporate
agile principles, feedback loops, and governance mechanisms, marking a clear shift from static, tool-based procedures
toward dynamic, learning-oriented systems aligned with strategic and human-centered objectives.

This transition is equally evident in academic governance contexts, where process mining and CRISP-DM-based
curriculum analysis frameworks illustrate the integration of data analytics and institutional management under shared
governance principles (Herrera Franco et al., 2025). Empirical implementations, such as the agile–CRISP-DM hybrid
applied in supply chain analytics (Hamdani et al., 2024), exemplify how iterative cycles foster organizational learning,
stakeholder engagement, and adaptive project management within real operational contexts. Within this shift, the
unified framework also provides scalable guidance for small and medium-sized enterprises, enabling them to adopt
agile analytics lifecycles that preserve methodological rigor while accommodating limited resources and rapidly

**5.1** **Theoretical Implications**


From a theoretical standpoint, the proposed framework advances the conceptual architecture of analytics
methodologies by merging process standardization with adaptive design principles. It extends classical models such
as CRISP-DM and ASUM-DM by embedding governance and continuous improvement as structural elements rather
than as auxiliary components. This synthesis aligns with international standards such as ISO/IEC 29110 and IEEE
7000, emphasizing iterative feedback, traceability, and ethical oversight. By linking technical processes with
managerial and governance layers, the framework strengthens the theoretical convergence of data science, software
engineering, and organizational management—establishing analytics as a mature interdisciplinary domain rather than
a collection of isolated practices.


**5.2** **Bridging Theory to Practice**


The theoretical contributions outlined above translate directly into practical value through the framework’s
modular and iterative structure. It serves as a bridge between academic rigor and organizational application,
demonstrating how governance, adaptability, and transparency can be operationalized across diverse project contexts.
Each phase is designed to scale with organizational maturity, regulatory requirements, and technological complexity.
By situating governance and feedback mechanisms within every stage of the analytics lifecycle, the framework
transforms abstract methodological principles into actionable guidance. This bridging perspective reinforces the
continuity between conceptual development and managerial execution, eliminating the traditional divide between
research-based methodologies and real-world implementation. The unified framework operationalizes the agile and
traceability principles central to MAISTRO by embedding iterative feedback, documentation control, and continuous
validation as governance mechanisms applicable beyond AI contexts.


**5.3** **Practical Implications**


Although this study is conceptual in scope, the unified framework could be illustrated through an empirical
adaptation similar to Bemthuis et al. (2025), who embedded CRISP-DM phases into the assessment of agent-based
simulation models using process mining. Compared to prior domain-specific frameworks—such as the HR analytics
model by Gurusinghe et al. (2021)—our framework is designed for broader cross-sector applicability. Following this
precedent, the proposed governance-aware structure could be applied to validate analytics projects by aligning
iterative feedback loops, documentation control, and evaluation mechanisms with real-world data workflows. Such

For practitioners and project sponsors, adopting this unified framework offers tangible managerial benefits. It helps

communication, and embedding continuous learning cycles within project governance. In practice, the framework
provides a roadmap to avoid trial-and-error and siloed execution, promoting consistency, accountability, and

In practice, the framework provides organizations with a structured framework for designing, executing, and

**5.4** **Summary of Implications**


In summary, the unified framework consolidates decades of methodological evolution into a single, adaptable
structure that strengthens the scientific, managerial, and ethical foundations of analytics project management. By
combining theoretical rigor with practical guidance, it enables consistent governance, scalability, and transparency
across sectors. The synthesis reinforces the professionalization of analytics practice and provides a foundation for
future empirical validation and refinement. These insights also establish a natural transition to the following section,
which outlines the study’s limitations and identifies future pathways for validation and methodological advancement.

**6** **Limitations**


The proposed PRODIG8 framework synthesizes decades of methodological development, yet several limitations
must be acknowledged. These constraints define priorities for future empirical validation and refinement.


    - Scope limitations: The analysis relies on a corpus of 18 documents retrieved exclusively from the Scopus
database. While Scopus provides extensive coverage, it may exclude relevant methodologies published
in non-indexed outlets, industry reports, or proprietary materials. Consequently, the study may not capture
all practitioner-oriented or emergent frameworks.

    - Methodological limitations: PRODIG8 is analytically derived from published methodological
frameworks rather than from observations of completed analytics projects. Although the sequential
structural-reconciliation procedure and subsequent mapping of methodological recommendations provide
a systematic basis for model construction, the resulting architecture has not yet been empirically evaluated
in real-world project environments. Moreover, differences in terminology, granularity, and scope across
the reviewed methodologies required interpretive judgments to determine conceptual equivalence, split
or merge functions, and map recommendations to the final dimensions.

    - Temporal limitations: The synthesis reflects the state of the analytics landscape at the time of data
collection. It may require revision as the field evolves—particularly with advances in automation,
explainable AI, and data-governance mechanisms. For example, the rapid adoption of generative AI and
autonomous decision systems may redefine governance expectations.

    - Language limitations: The restriction to English-language sources may exclude non-English
methodological contributions.

    - Analytical subjectivity: Constructing PRODIG8 necessarily involved interpretive decisions during both
structural reconciliation and recommendation mapping. Two reviewers made these decisions
systematically and independently and then reconciled them through consensus; nevertheless, alternative
interpretations of methodological components remain possible. Future research can therefore complement
the present analytical derivation through external expert assessment, structured applications to completed
analytics projects, and empirical examination of the relationships represented in the model.

    - Lack of validation: Although conceptual, future validation efforts could include expert panels,
longitudinal case studies, or structured mappings of published projects to the framework’s phases. These

Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for empirical validation and longitudinal studies to evaluate the
framework’s applicability, scalability, and practical relevance across diverse organizational contexts and industries.
They also define priorities for methodological refinement and provide a foundation for the research directions
discussed in the following section.


**7** **Conclusions**


Building on the sequential reconciliation and consolidation of 18 analytics-project methodologies, this study
develops PRODIG8 as an integrated architecture for the systematic execution, governance, and adaptation of business
analytics projects. The model comprises eight interdependent methodological dimensions with three distinct
functional roles. Project Scope Definition, Data Understanding, Data Preparation, Project Design, Model Evaluation,
and Operation and Maintenance constitute the core execution lifecycle; Governance and Ethics provides transversal
control across that lifecycle; and Continuous Improvement provides the adaptive feedback mechanism through which
project results, operational experience, stakeholder feedback, and changing conditions can inform preceding activities.
This execution–control–adaptation architecture extends methodological integration beyond the consolidation of
lifecycle stages by explicitly differentiating the functions through which analytics projects are executed, governed,
and progressively refined. PRODIG8 thereby provides a common analytical structure for organizing and examining
analytics-project practices across organizational and application contexts.

While conceptually robust, the framework remains theoretical and requires empirical validation. Future research
should therefore test and operationalize it in real-world contexts through longitudinal case studies, cross-sector
applications, or controlled experiments to assess its impact on project outcomes, scalability, and stakeholder
engagement. An additional validation pathway involves educational analytics and curriculum management, where
CRISP-DM-based process mining models provide an ideal context to test the unified framework’s adaptability and its
governance-oriented structure (Herrera Franco et al., 2025). Additional efforts could develop maturity assessment
tools and performance indicators that evaluate organizational capabilities for implementing analytics initiatives in
accordance with the proposed phases. Future empirical validation should focus on engineering and sensor-intensive
contexts to test how the unified framework operationalizes DMME’s technical phases within real production
environments and cyber-physical systems. A promising validation pathway involves hybrid applications that combine
CRISP-DM with agile project management approaches, as illustrated by Hamdani et al. (2024), which demonstrate
how iterative sprints and stakeholder-driven learning cycles operationalize methodological integration in complex
supply chain environments. In particular, further studies should explore how the unified governance-aware framework
can function as an agile SDLC model for Big Data Analytics Systems in small and medium-sized organizations,
extending the comparative foundations established by Salazar-Salazar et al. (2024) and validating its adaptability

under resource constraints.


As analytics ecosystems evolve toward AI-augmented, automated, and explainable systems, continued inquiry
should embed continuous improvement, interpretability, and governance mechanisms into adaptive, data-driven
environments. Such research will not only strengthen the empirical foundation of the framework but also guide the
emergence of future governance-oriented analytics systems that integrate accountability, transparency, and ethical
oversight as core design principles.

From a practical standpoint, PRODIG8 provides a structured analytical reference for examining the completeness
and coherence of an organization’s approach to analytics-project execution. The six core execution dimensions enable
practitioners to examine whether activities spanning project definition, data understanding and preparation, analytical
design, evaluation, and operational maintenance are adequately represented, while the transversal Governance and
Ethics dimension directs attention to accountability, transparency, risk, and ethical oversight throughout those
activities. Continuous Improvement complements these functions by requiring project results, operational experience,
stakeholder feedback, and changing conditions to be systematically reconsidered across the execution lifecycle.
PRODIG8 can therefore be used not only to organize project activities, roles, and deliverables, but also to identify
methodological areas that are absent, weakly represented, or insufficiently connected within an existing project
approach. In this way, the model provides a common architecture for comparing, reviewing, and refining analyticsproject practices while preserving the adaptability required across organizational contexts.

Ultimately, this study lays the groundwork for bridging analytics theory and practice, fostering a mature,
standardized, and ethically grounded discipline capable of evolving into the next generation of governance-aware,
adaptive analytics systems. In the long term, the proposed framework can inform the development of institutional
standards, accreditation guidelines, maturity models, and methodological curricula in analytics education and
professional certification, fostering convergence between academic research, industry practice, and governance
systems. In this sense, the unified framework offers a solid foundation for developing governance-aware standards
that can inform and harmonize the next generation of analytics methodologies. In methodological terms, this
progression extends the lineage initiated by DMME, transferring its engineering-focused rigor to a broader
governance-aware framework that now encompasses business, institutional, and cross-sector analytics domains. To
enhance its empirical robustness, future research should validate the framework within real-world organizational
contexts, assessing both implementation outcomes and stakeholder perceptions. By unifying methodological,
managerial, and ethical dimensions, this study not only advances analytics methodology but also provides an
actionable foundation for evidence-based governance, making it a relevant contribution to both scholarly research and
applied data science practice.

In summary, PRODIG8 provides a unifying foundation that connects decades of methodological evolution with
contemporary governance and ethical standards. Its adoption can catalyze the transition of analytics from a fragmented
set of practices into a mature, standardized discipline—offering both scholars and practitioners a common
methodological language for designing, governing, and improving analytics initiatives.
