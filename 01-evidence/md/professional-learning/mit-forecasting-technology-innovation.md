###### Why Is Forecasting Technology Innovation Important

Decision makers who are skilled at forecasting technology innovation can have a competitive

advantage.


Investors can stay ahead of industry trends and build a successful technology investment

portfolio.


Scientists and engineers can better prioritize resources invested in technology research

and development.


Companies can be more successful at anticipating and entering new and promising

markets.


Governments can support national competitiveness and advance technology for the
benefit of society.


The better these decision makers are at measuring and forecasting technology innovation,
the more effective their decision-making processes around technology investment can be.

###### Forecasting technology innovation for a competitive advantage

“At the outset, let us dismiss a source of great confusion

concerning technological forecasting—that is, the purpose

of this activity. To be useful, technological forecasts do not
necessarily need to predict the precise form technology
will take in a given application at some specific future date.

Like any other forecasts, their purpose is simply to help
evaluate the probability and significance of various

possible future developments so that managers can make

better decisions.’’


(Quinn, 2014)


“Measuring innovation is key to achieving tangible

results. Establishing viable innovation metrics is

essential to bringing leaders on board, lining up the

resources and attracting the talented colleagues you

need to turn ideas into done deals.’’


(Tucker, 2021)


“Technology forecasting is strategically both a defensive
and offensive activity. It can assist in resource allocation
and minimize the adverse impacts or maximize the

favorable impacts of game-changing technology trends.”


(National Research Council, 2010)

###### Industry Applications

Any organization looking to gain a competitive advantage by improving existing
technologies and implementing new ones can benefit from an advanced, data-informed

decision-making process. This course will be particularly relevant to:


Technology innovation is essential to the evolving healthcare

industry. New technologies involving AI, data science, and
analytics are poised to improve various facets of healthcare.

For example, to eliminate future supply chain disruptions 50% of
life science companies and healthcare providers are set to boost

investments in AI and advanced analytics (Limaye, 2021).

However, questions remain about how to successfully implement

these new technologies within existing processes, and which

technologies to prioritize. Successful technology innovation
strategies promise to further understanding of the genetic factors

behind disease, optimize clinical studies and trials, and

streamline regulatory approval processes.


86% of manufacturers in the US have stated that smart factories

and their associated technologies would be the most important

drivers of competition by 2025 (Laaper et al., 2020). The

extensive list of potential supporting technologies for this
transition— including IoT, data analytics, cloud computing, and

machine learning and AI, among others—highlights the need for a

data-driven, selective approach to technology investment and

design in a sector marked by heightened competition. If equipped
with the expertise and tools necessary to predict and influence

the evolution of these technologies, organizations stand to gain a

substantial advantage over their competitors.

Investing in technology innovation means working with

uncertainty. While no crystal ball exists, data-driven forecasting
models and mechanistic models of innovation can point to fast

improving industries and particularly promising technologies

within them. This information is essential for balancing portfolios
of investments to achieve success. Whether investments are

being made in early-stage technologies or more advanced ones, it

is important to recognize both trends and noise in the

development of technologies to appropriately diversify or

concentrate investments. Using data-informed methods to
measure and forecast technological change, investment firms
can gain a significant advantage


The global energy market is experiencing a rapid transition driven

by fundamental changes in technological development,

regulations, consumer preferences, and investor sentiments. The

everKgrowing pressure to reduce greenhouse gas emissions

through domestic and international policy and through corporate
sustainability programs is having a significant impact on the

choices of technologies that serve the energy sector.

Simultaneously, the shifting of energy portfolios through
increased electrification, emerging alternate fuel and energy
storage options, and efficiency gains are further advancing the

need for new and innovative technology solutions. The challenge

is to be armed with the best tools to understand and act on the
most effective energy options to meet short and long-term market
demands.

(McKinsey, 2022).

The chemical industry is directly or indirectly involved in the

manufacturing of nearly every product, and is currently

undergoing a shift as its business processes and models become

digitized. Concurrently, calls for increased circular economy

practices and more sustainable operations are emerging from an
array of industries, offering the chemical industry major

new opportunities for growth. On the other hand, each individual

organization will need to be prepared to take a proactive—rather

than reactive—approach to technology innovation and
implementation, using available data to weigh opportunities and
risks; make effective, informed decisions; and gain a competitive
advantage. Data-informed models can help businesses benefit

from advances in technology, including through improved supply
chain management, preventative maintenance, and other

cost-reduction strategies.


With the ability to measure and track technological progress,

public policymakers can analyze and promote technology
innovations in terms of societal goals. This ability to understand
and influence the drivers behind technology advancements helps

to remove the guesswork from the process of choosing and
developing the right technologies, offering governments insights

into how money and time can be invested to best meet their

objectives.

###### Participant Profile

Although the expertise found throughout this course can be applied to a wide
series of industries, it is especially beneficial for those working in sectors such
as chemicals, life sciences, manufacturing, investment, energy, and public policy.
Though not limited to any job level, the most common roles that will benefit from

the course include:


Research and development managers looking to advance their organization’s

technology portfolio decision-making through data-informed insights.


Production or manufacturing operations managers who wish to incorporate
data collection and analysis to anticipate technological changes and prioritize

their investments in innovation


C-suite and executive level management in technology-related firms in charge of

establishing a data-informed technology plan for their organization.

technological progress can be measured and predicted to help meet societal

goals.


Private investors interested in optimizing technology-related portfolios based on
technology performance data, allowing them to improve technology investment

decisions.

###### Instructor

Prof. Jessika Trancik


Professor in the Institute for Data, Systems, and

Society, MIT


Jessika Trancik is a professor in the Institute

for Data, Systems, and Society at the

Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Her

research examines the impacts of

technologies and the reasons behind

technological change.



She has developed theory and predictive

models to understand why some

technologies improve faster than others, and

what technology features enable rapid

innovation. Trancik has developed models

for forecasting technological change, which

inform engineering design, public policy, and

investment portfolios. Several of her theories

and models have been applied to new and

developing energy technologies, such as

solar energy and batteries, and to electricity

and transportation systems. Her models

have also been used to inform government

innovation policy, and applied in diverse
industries, including finance, healthcare,

manufacturing, software, and consumer

products. Her work has been published in

journals such as Nature, Proceedings of the

National Academy of Sciences, Nature

Energy, Nature Climate Change, and

Environmental Science and Technology, and

has been featured by news outlets such as

the New York Times, Washington Post,

Financial Times, and NPR.
