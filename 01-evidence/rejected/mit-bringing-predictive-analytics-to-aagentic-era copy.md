**Foreword**


**For most of the past decade, the competitive question in analytics was who could predict**

**better. That question is largely settled. The harder divide now separating leaders from**

**everyone else is who can act on a prediction autonomously, at scale, in real time, and**

**without drifting from business intent.**


**This is where most organizations quietly stall, and the reason is rarely the model. A system**

**can correctly predict that a customer is likely to churn or that a claim is worth a certain**

**amount, and still produce the wrong decision because it does not understand the process**

**it is supposed to operate within. That knowledge does not live in the data. It lives in the**

**operational depth of people who have run the process thousands of times.**


**Take collections. A model can rank customers by likelihood of payment. But the decision that**

**actually matters is more delicate. For example, one customer just had a payment fail for the**
**third time, they are in a jurisdiction with specific hardship protections, the last agent who**

**called them noted a recent bereavement, and the right move is not another payment**
**reminder but a pause and a different tone entirely. A frontline specialist who has handled**

**ten thousand of these conversations knows that instinctively. They know the ordinary path,**

**and, more importantly, the hundred corner cases where that path is exactly wrong. The**

**same logic applies in insurance claims assessment, fraud detection, and healthcare demand**

**forecasting. The domain changes, but the problem does not.**


**That accumulated judgment is the real asset. The routine cases, the difficult ones, and the**

**rare exceptions are precisely the material we use to train AI agents — so the system learns**

**not just the typical decision but the full distribution of situations a human expert has learned**

**to navigate. An agent trained only on the common path fails quietly when it meets the edge**

**case, and at scale, that failure compounds.**


**This report moves past the comfortable consensus that predictive AI is powerful, and onto**

**the harder question: what does it take to let these systems act? The answer is the depth of**

**process knowledge, corner cases, and all that gets encoded into the agents themselves.**

**And the gap between organizations that have that depth and those that don’t is widening**

**every quarter.**


Sebastian Cubela

Global Head of Data and AI Partnerships, TP

**Key takeaways**



Predictive analytics has shifted from
static forecasts to living systems —
ingesting unstructured data, updating in
real time, and surfacing insight through
natural-language interfaces.


Intelligent analytics have wide-ranging
applications across industries including
healthcare, financial services, and
manufacturing. But challenges around
data readiness, strategic alignment, talent,
and maintaining predictive models through
continuous improvement persist.


Few enterprises have the in-house breadth
to orchestrate the full stack — technology,
talent, governance, and industry-specific
use cases. Partnering is becoming the
default path to scaled AI, not a stopgap.

whether predictive models can outperform statistical

forecasts—that argument is settled. The big question

now is how to enable predictive systems to act on

autonomous decision making, and the gap between

“Enterprises are done with a backward-looking point


of view; they want to be more forward-thinking,” says
Vishal Gupta, partner at research firm Everest Group.


Intelligent analytics, powered by technologies like deep

learning and generative AI, are making this possible.

Real-time training allows AI to evolve continuously

instead of waiting for quarterly refreshes. In addition,

the data that newer predictive engines rely upon has

expanded to encompass not just neat, numerical

records but also messy, unstructured sources of

insight-rich interactions. As a result, AI-powered

analytics are moving enterprises from passive



How AI is reshaping predictive analytics
AI takes predictive analytics—a broad discipline that


includes predictive modeling, data prep, analysis
workflows, interpretation of results, and decision-making
applications—to new heights. “In many ways I think

the word ‘analytics’ is giving way to AI,” says Gupta.

“Everything is becoming AI.”


Several technological breakthroughs are to thank.

First is the ability of generative AI systems to ingest
unstructured data at scale. “[AI systems] can now

process text, voice, image, and streaming data, which

means real-time integration of inputs like sensors,

customer interaction data, and social media interactions,”

explains Gupta, noting that these previously untapped

goldmines often provide far richer and “situationally

aware” intel than a standard customer satisfaction

score (CSAT) or net promoter score (NPS).

The second breakthrough is the move from static,


infrequently-updated models to real-time intelligence.

Instead of waiting weeks for an analyst to refresh a

dashboard, AI models now learn continuously from

live data streams.

A third development pertains to user interfaces. Past


iterations of predictive technologies were often clunky

for the average frontline employee with minimal data

analysis expertise to navigate. “The interface was just
not [practical],” says Pedro Amorim, co-founder of
consulting firm LTP Labs and a professor teaching
data-driven decision making at the University of Porto

in Portugal. Users can engage with today’s generative

AI systems via intuitive natural-language commands.


Classification of data, too, has become more streamlined.
Danny Kuivenhoven, chief technology officer for EMEA
and APAC at TP, gives an example in the context of
customer interactions. “When classification becomes
consistent at scale, something bigger happens—you

stop looking at customer interactions as conversations

and start seeing them as a structured signal you can

actually act on. That’s the moment predictive analytics

changes from a reporting tool into an operating system

for the business,” says Kuivenhoven.


“Enterprises are no

longer treating predictive
analytics and automation
as separate initiatives.
Instead, they’re creating
closed-loop decision
systems. Predictive
analytics uses the data,
be it real-time, structured,
or unstructured, to create
a set of insights based
on which agentic AI can
make certain autonomous
decisions.”


**Vishal Gupta**, Partner, Everest Group



At scale, this capability also becomes a powerful lens for

operational insight. We can get almost a FinOps-level

operation showing a breakdown of volumes and

associated costs. For instance, ‘This month you spent
$250,000 on returns, and this month we spent $100,000

on payment questions,’” Kuivenhoven continues. Such
findings can more accurately feed predictive models
and help organizations identify emerging cost drivers
or proactively adjust staffing and workflows.


The final piece of the puzzle, notes Gupta, is integration.

More and more organizations are now embedding

predictive capabilities directly into automated
workflows—and the rise of AI agents will almost
certainly accelerate this trend.


Danny Kuivenhoven, chief technology officer for EMEA
and APAC at TP, provides an example of how multimodal

data can add new dimensions to predictive insight:

Smart video systems that blend predictive analytics

with computer vision and generative AI. “We can see a

problem through users’ mobile phone cameras, and now

we can actually do something directly with the images,”

he says.


In car insurance claims, for example, this capability

allows organizations to move beyond historical averages.

Instead, AI systems can analyze visual inputs alongside

structured information to assess damage in real time.

“We can create a predictive model based on the type


of car, the age of the vehicle, and the severity of the

damage, and we can estimate the potential value of

that claim,” explains Kuivenhoven.


Despite these advances, human oversight remains

essential, particularly when models are making highstakes financial calls. Visual data can be ambiguous,
and edge cases are inevitable. “That’s why it’s important

to have human annotators review and correct the

algorithm as needed,” he says.

Wide-ranging industry applications

Kuivenhoven sees this shift as a fundamental expansion

of predictive analytics’ role. “Previously, predictive

capabilities focused primarily on what was happening

next, but now intelligent prediction is meeting action.”

He points to several recent client engagements:


- In B2B sales, a multinational food and beverage services


provider hoped to use AI to read buying patterns more

accurately and better anticipate churn. To address this

need, TP built out propensity and segmentation models

based on three years of interaction data, grouping

accounts by order size and frequency. Internal data

from the company shows results of the initiative: 11%

more sales tickets, a 27% rise in average revenue per

ticket, 70% more accurate churn forecasts, and a 41%

gain in NPS. At a US technology company, the same


interaction-analytics approach automated 100% of

quality audits and cut repeat calls by roughly a quarter,

which from internal TP data shows a 38% lift in sales

conversion.


- Collections, in which AI models decide not only who to


contact, but when and how often based on their prior

behavior, is another promising area of application. To

put this into practice for a US-based retailer trying

to outperform a rival vendor on past-due accounts
before write-off, TP scored each account for likelihood
of payment. Internal TP data shows an 11.2% higher

liquidation rate than the competing vendor, plus $1.8

million in additional recoveries over nine months.


- Hiring is a newer use case for predictive analytics.


To assist a hospitality company in the UK struggling


with sales targets and early turnover, TP trained

machine learning models on psychometric results
and the profiles of top-performing agents to identify
the factors predicting success, then used the models

to inform a new hiring campaign. Kuivenhoven says

agents selected this way raised average sales per

ticket by 55% and were 40% less likely to leave

the organization.

Building the foundation for AI
orchestration at scale

Despite the hype around AI adoption, hurdles remain. And

many of the real challenges are organizational rather than

purely technical. Data readiness, for example, is an

ongoing process that many companies misjudge as a
one-time effort. “Your data is never going to be in an
‘optimal’ state. Knowing that, what you need to ask is,

‘Which data do I really need to get ready?’” says Amorim.
“For that, you need to answer another question first:

‘What are the business cases that I really want to solve?’”


Equally important is ensuring strategic alignment

before tacking on technological bells and whistles.

Gupta emphasizes the importance of outlining clear

key performance indicators tied to business outcomes

ahead of any predictive AI implementations. Without

such alignment, analytics projects risk becoming

proof-of-concept exercises that never translate into

return on investment.

A commitment to quality assurance is another core

tenet. Kuivenhoven notes that a focus on continuous

improvement is an often-overlooked factor in maintaining

predictive models’ potency. A commitment to quality

assurance will be even more critical as AI starts to take
actions on its own. “The biggest flaw I see today in
autonomous systems is that there’s no form of continuous

improvement,” Kuivenhoven says. “Many vendors simply
conduct periodical reviews on quality and intervene [only
when it has obviously degraded].” This can translate to

growing erosion of user trust or decisions that quietly

drift away from business objectives.


The next challenge is talent. The expanding AI ecosystem


covers everything from generative and descriptive AI to

prescriptive and simulation-based approaches, and

organizations need people who can navigate those

overlapping ideas. “You really need to know at least a

little bit about all these different AI technologies, because
otherwise you might be using a hammer to nail in a screw,”

says Amorim.


Finally, sustainable impact requires cross-functional

collaboration. Gupta cautions against siloed IT-led projects,
stressing that the talent/team structure for deploying AI

needs to be a holistic process. “It cannot just be the tech

teams; it cannot just be the domain teams,” he emphasizes.


For many organizations, the breadth of expertise

required to get the most out of AI’s predictive capabilities

makes a strong case for partnering with a service

provider that has already navigated these complexities.
Such partners can offer what might be called “AI
orchestration.” a multifaceted approach that combines

the right technology, people, and proven, industryspecific use cases to optimize results.


Responsible implementation
and governance
Disciplined data governance is another cornerstone of
effective predictive AI adoption, especially for industries
dealing with sensitive data. “In the market today, there’s

a big maturity gap with AI. Companies that are low on

that scale and experimenting with entry-level AI are

often relying heavily on the guardrailing that the major


large language model providers include,” says Kuiven­

hoven. As organizations move toward more specialized
use cases—and begin training or fine-tuning models
using proprietary data—that level of built-in protection
is no longer sufficient.



**Synthetic data as a**
**catalyst for predictive**
**modeling innovation**


Clean and compliant data is perhaps the most important
component of effective predictive modeling, but this
precious resource can be both costly to obtain and

fraught with privacy concerns. In regulated industries in

particular, customer interaction records or health data

may be too sensitive to use freely. In other cases like

simulating an anomalous event or disruption, the right
data simply might not exist in sufficient volume.


This is where the value of synthetic data—artificially
generated datasets that mirror the statistical properties

of real-world information—is starting to become clear.

Such datasets help address the twofold challenge of

data scarcity and data sensitivity.


Synthetic data can be created and refined in a few
different ways:


- Algorithms generate new records based on patterns


learned from actual datasets


- Simulation engines replicate real-world processes,

such as traffic flows or patient care pathways, to create
plausible test scenarios


- Data augmentation techniques add variation to existing


records (e.g., altering geolocations or word choices in

customer transcripts) to broaden model training sets


At TP, synthetic data is standard practice for

development and experimentation. “Anything new

that we build, we start with synthetic data, test that it

works, and then slowly move into real live data to see if

we’re getting the same results,” says Kuivenhoven. The

approach also supports guardrailing and compliance

testing. Synthetic datasets make it easier to verify

that monitoring systems trigger the right alerts before

deploying in production environments.


Still, generating high-quality synthetic data requires

careful methodology to avoid introducing new biases

or simply replicating the blind spots of existing

models. For organizations without deep data science

expertise, partnering with a provider experienced in

these techniques can accelerate experimentation while

minimizing risk.

Effective governance begins with robust redaction and
anonymization practices to ensure customer information

is protected throughout the analytics lifecycle. Truly

responsible governance also requires thinking beyond
the back-office; it necessitates direct client involvement to

establish what data can be collected, how it will be used,

and under what safeguards. In regulated industries such
as healthcare and finance, these guardrails often define
whether an AI initiative can scale beyond pilot programs.


The tumultuous regulatory landscape adds urgency to

governance efforts. Gupta believes pressure will only
mount as more laws like the EU Artificial Intelligence Act
take effect. “In the coming months and years, the EU’s
AI regulations will come into full force. Enterprises are


increasingly considering this in their technology systems,

but also in their governance processes. Who can access

what data? Where is this data being used, and in what

kind of models?” he says.


In terms of early best practices, many enterprises are

deploying “walled gardens” for AI, where proprietary or

sensitive data is isolated and processed within secure,

controlled environments. Documentation and testing

protocols are becoming standard, ensuring models can

be explained and audited.


Moving forward, the emphasis will likely be on making

governance frameworks more proactive and scalable.

Synthetic data and advanced cleaning techniques will

help reduce bias and limit reliance on raw personal

data while preserving analytical accuracy. Meanwhile,

human-in-the-loop approaches will continue to provide

oversight at critical junctures.

Looking forward
AI is, of course, still not an oracle; it cannot guarantee


business outcomes. But with every leap forward in

model design and computational power, its ability to

deliver timely, actionable insights grows more impres­

sive. In the context of predictive analytics, this means

the days of linear projections are coming to an end.

In its place, an era of reliable, living systems is

taking shape.


Success with this new paradigm will depend on the

balance organizations strike between machine

intelligence and human judgment. Gupta observes that

we are on the cusp of a major cultural shift, one in which

both AI systems and human domain experts will guide
how effective new predictive models can be.


“It’s a one-way street now with AI, but one thing is very


clear: The future is human-AI collaboration,” he says.

“There will be tasks that will be completely taken over by


AI and tasks that will be completely done by humans, but

the biggest chunk of all activities will involve humans
plus-AI coming together in varying degrees. That’s the

future we are headed for, and the sooner enterprises

and organizations realize it, the faster they can gain

competitive advantage.”


That collaborative ethos must also shape how


organizations think about outside expertise. As the

ecosystem continues to evolve, few organizations will

have the internal resources to keep pace with all the

moving parts of AI orchestration on their own. Partnering

with providers experienced in these complexities will be a
defining factor for realizing the technology’s true potential.
